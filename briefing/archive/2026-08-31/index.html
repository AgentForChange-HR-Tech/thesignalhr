What changed this week — and what it means

Six signals worth your attention, each with the practitioner's "so what." Read in five minutes; sound informed for a week.

Curated by Alex RiveraUpdated September 2, 2026Refreshed weekly
Region
RegulationAmericas

California passes the "No Robo Bosses Act" — Newsom has until September 30

SB 947 cleared the Senate 28–10 on August 31 after a 53–14 Assembly vote, and now sits on the governor's desk. If signed, it would be the first US law to bar employers from relying solely on automated decision systems to fire or discipline workers, require human oversight and verification when ADS assists those decisions, and oblige employers to tell workers when ADS was used. Enforcement runs through the labor commissioner, attorney general, and local prosecutors — there is no private right of action. Business groups opposed it; the California Labor Federation sponsored it.

So what · Don't wait for the signature to find out whether you're in scope. If any performance, attendance, productivity, or conduct tool in your stack feeds a discipline or termination workflow for California employees, map it now and name the human who signs off. The "human in the loop" needs to be real, documented, and able to override — a rubber stamp won't satisfy "verification."

Sources: Sen. McNerney (official release) · Epstein Becker Green · GovTech

RegulationAmericas

Thirty days out: Connecticut's AI law switches on October 1 — and "the algorithm did it" stops being a defense

Public Act 26-15 (the CART Act) starts phasing in on October 1, 2026. From that date, using automated employment-decision technology is explicitly not a defense to a discrimination claim in Connecticut (evidence of anti-bias testing can mitigate), and any WARN notice for a plant closing or mass layoff must state whether the cuts relate to AI or other technological change. The heavier notice regime — plain-language disclosure of the tool's trade name, purpose, data categories, and sources before any AI-influenced decision — follows on October 1, 2027. Colorado's pared-back law now lands January 1, 2027.

So what · Two things to do this month if you have Connecticut headcount: confirm your last bias audit is recent enough to cite as mitigation, and add an "AI-related?" checkbox to your RIF planning template — you'll be answering that question on a government form. Nationally, note the pattern: Connecticut, Colorado, and California all converge on notice, human review, and vendor-doesn't-carry-liability. Build once to the strictest.

Sources: Ogletree Deakins · Fisher Phillips · Littler (Colorado)

WorkforceAmericas

NY Fed: AI use jumped to 61% of service firms — and only 4% laid anyone off because of it

The New York Fed's August regional business surveys, published September 1, found AI adoption climbing to 61% of service firms (from 40% in 2025) and 51% of manufacturers (from 26%). Yet just 4% of service firms and zero manufacturers reported AI-driven layoffs in the past six months. About 15% of service firms hired fewer people than they otherwise would have; 13% hired more to help them use AI. Investment is shallow — three-quarters of service firms call their AI spend "minimal to modest," and the median adopter has only 17% of workers using it. Retraining, not replacement, remains the dominant workforce response.

So what · This is the third data set in six weeks (after Ramp–Revelio and Gallup) saying the same thing: at the firm level, AI is reshaping work more than it is cutting jobs — so far. The 15% "hired fewer" figure is the number to watch; it's how AI shows up in headcount plans without ever appearing in a layoff announcement. If your workforce plan assumes AI-driven reductions, check whether the mechanism is real attrition or just slower backfill, and budget the retraining line accordingly.

Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of New York — Liberty Street Economics

TalentGlobal

EY puts $100M behind the judgment premium — cash bonuses for the skills AI can't fake

EY US will spend $100 million rewarding employees who demonstrate "future-focused" human skills — judgment, business acumen, adaptability, collaboration, and experimentation with AI. Spot awards run up to $500; team and individual awards for work that "makes a material difference" run $10,000–$25,000, with no cap per person and peer nomination at any level. Notably, the firm's own press release never uses the words "artificial intelligence." The Wall Street Journal broke the story; Fortune and CBS followed on September 1.

So what · This is the first major employer to put a price on the judgment premium we wrote about in July — and to do it through compensation rather than a competency framework nobody reads. The design is copyable: small, frequent, peer-nominated awards for observable behaviors. Before you build one, define what "judgment" looks like in your context specifically enough that a manager can point to it — otherwise you've created a popularity bonus. Our analysis of why critical thinking now outranks AI skills.

Sources: Fortune · CBS News

ProductGlobal

"Stop buying software, start hiring AI employees" — Ema ships a hire-to-retire agent hub

Ema launched an HR, IT and Finance Hub on September 1: pre-trained agents that run onboarding, benefits, headcount planning, access provisioning, payroll, and expense workflows end-to-end across an enterprise's existing HRIS, identity, and finance systems, with role-based permissions, audit trails, and human sign-off on flagged decisions. Reference customer Wipro reports roughly 2.9 million employee queries a year handled through the platform and 100+ automated workflows. The same day, UKG's CIO told Fortune the company's own 14,000 employees have built more than 12,000 internal agents and 387 AI apps, which UKG credits with 8,500 hours of monthly productivity.

So what · Agents are arriving from two directions at once — packaged "AI employee" catalogs from vendors, and thousands of home-grown agents your own staff are building in Copilot, Gemini, and ChatGPT. The governance question is the same for both: who owns the agent, what systems can it write to, and where is the log? If you can't answer that for the agents already inside your walls, you're not ready to buy more. Start with an inventory, not an RFP.

Sources: GlobeNewswire (Ema) · Fortune — CIO Intelligence

MarketGlobal

Trust is the ROI multiplier: governed AI is 15× more likely to pay off — and agents trail on trust

An IDC study commissioned by SAS, surveying 2,699 decision-makers across 28 countries, finds organizations with "trustworthy AI" practices — data quality and governance, model oversight, explainability, responsible-AI policy, and audit — are 15 times more likely to report strong ROI from AI projects. Trust drops from 76% for generative AI to 66% for agentic AI, even though 89% of respondents say their agents already make some autonomous decisions. And 79% of organizations report employees overriding AI outputs in more than 10% of cases, citing missing explanations, biased outputs, and factual errors.

So what · The override rate is the metric your HR AI vendor isn't showing you. Ask for it: how often do recruiters, managers, or HRBPs reject the tool's recommendation, and why? A high override rate on a screening or scheduling agent is either a trust problem or an accuracy problem — both are your problem under the new state laws above. Fold "explainability at the decision level" into every scorecard this renewal cycle; the ROI data says it's not a compliance tax, it's the thing that makes the investment work.

Sources: PR Newswire (IDC/SAS) · Fortune
