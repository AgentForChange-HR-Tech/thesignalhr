What changed this week — and what it means

Six signals worth your attention, each with the practitioner's "so what." Read in five minutes; sound informed for a week.

Curated by Alex RiveraUpdated September 14, 2026Refreshed weekly
Region
RegulationAmericas

DHS moves to end the 60-day H-1B grace period — a layoff of a visa holder could mean no cushion at all

On September 11 the Department of Homeland Security published a proposed rule eliminating the discretionary 60-day grace period that has let H-1B, L-1, O-1, E and TN workers stay in the US to find new employment after losing a job. In place since 2016, the grace period is now described by DHS as an administrative "burden" that is "inconsistent with the purpose" of the visa categories. A 10-day grace period would remain, but only after the visa's validity period ends — so, as immigration firm Reddy Neumann Brown notes, an H-1B employee laid off two years into a three-year approval would have no cushion. Employers stay liable for return transportation costs. Comments are open for 60 days, until November 10.

So what · This lands on the same desks as the AI-restructuring question. If any 2027 workforce plan touches roles held by H-1B or similar visa holders, model the rule as if final: a redundancy notice becomes a departure notice, portability becomes a race, and your relocation and severance policies may need a visa-specific track. Two things to do before November 10: pull a count of affected employees by visa type and expiry date, and decide with counsel whether to file a comment — trade groups will, and employer data on mobility costs carries weight. Pair this with last week's SB 951 90-day AI-layoff notice: for a California employer, the two rules together turn "who is affected" into the first question in any restructure.

Sources: HR Dive · Federal Register (NPRM) · Reddy Neumann Brown

ProductGlobal

Salesforce gives its agents names and job titles — "Paige" now answers your employees' HR questions in Slack

Ten days after leading HiBob's $166M round, Salesforce unveiled seven named, role-specific Agentforce agents on September 11: Hunter, Piper and Carter for sales and commerce, Casey and Fin for service, Marshall for supply chain, and Paige, an internal help-desk agent that fields employees' HR and IT requests through Slack and internal portals. Six are generally available now; Hunter, which carries a new "long-horizon runtime" that lets an agent hold a plan across weeks and sessions, is in pilot until November. A companion Agentforce Coworker update adds multi-agent orchestration and an "Agent Script" syntax for forcing agents to execute sensitive steps a prescribed way.

So what · The HR-tech question isn't whether Paige is good; it's where she sits. Salesforce is positioning the employee help desk — the front door to HR — inside Slack, with HiBob as a likely system of record behind it. If your HRIS vendor also sells an HR agent (Workday, SAP, Oracle, UKG all do), you now have two candidates for the same tier-0 experience, and only one can own the conversation log. Decide the front door deliberately: which agent employees talk to, which system holds the transcript, and what happens when Paige needs to write to payroll. And read the long-horizon runtime as a preview — agents that remember across weeks are agents that accumulate employee context, which is exactly what your data-retention policy was written without.

Sources: SiliconANGLE · Salesforce Newsroom · Unite.AI

MarketGlobal

SAP promises 400 agents this month — and its own research says 3% of companies are ready for them

Speaking at Goldman Sachs' Communacopia & Technology conference, SAP CEO Christian Klein said the company will ship 400 AI agents during September with a second wave in the autumn — the first hard number investors have been given, though with no breakdown by product line. The same week, SAP's "Value of AI" study with Oxford Economics (2,600 leaders, 13 countries) reported that only 3% of businesses globally consider themselves fully prepared to deploy and scale agents, 78% doubt their upskilling is keeping pace with AI, and, in the Canadian cut, 46% of firms using agentic AI have no human-in-the-loop review at all. September Patch Day also brought a CVSS 10.0 vulnerability (CVE-2026-44756) in an SAP passport-processing component. SAP shares are down 16% year to date.

So what · Four hundred agents is a catalog, not a roadmap. For a SuccessFactors customer, the useful question for your account team is which of those agents can write to Employee Central, what approval step sits in front of each, and whether they're licensed in your existing Joule entitlement or metered separately. The vendor's own numbers hand you the governance argument: if 97% of organizations say they're not ready, "we'll turn on the agents when we have the review process" is a defensible position, not a laggard one. And route the Patch Day note to whoever owns your SAP basis team — HR data is the payload.

Sources: ad-hoc-news (Klein at Communacopia) · HRD America (SAP / Oxford Economics study)

TalentGlobal

ManpowerGroup: hiring outlook up for Q4, but AI hasn't moved time-to-hire — 29% say it got slower

ManpowerGroup's Q4 2026 Employment Outlook Survey puts the global net employment outlook at 29%, up two points on the quarter and six on the year; the US reads 36%, with 43% of employers globally planning to add headcount between October and December and only 14% expecting cuts. The finding that matters for talent teams: despite near-universal AI adoption in recruiting, 41% of employers say their average time-to-hire is unchanged from last year, 28% say it's faster, and 29% say it's slower. The top brake is a lack of candidates with the required skills; the top accelerator is better candidate matching. Sixty-two percent of employers adding headcount say it's because roles and skills are changing, not because of growth.

So what · If your AI screening vendor promised a faster funnel and your time-to-hire is flat, you're in the majority — and the data says the bottleneck moved, not disappeared. AI clears the top of the funnel faster, and then the candidates aren't there. Two implications: measure time-to-hire by stage so you can see where the days went, and put the "62% hiring for changed roles" number in front of the L&D budget owner — a role that's changing is a role you can fill from inside faster than from a thin market. Our analysis of which skills the market is actually paying for.

Sources: HRD America · ManpowerGroup Q4 2026 MEOS

WorkforceGlobal

Gallagher: 51% of HR functions use AI "in a limited way," 3% extensively — and retention is now the top priority for 57%

Gallagher's 2026 US Workforce Trends Report, drawing on more than 3,700 respondents, finds HR's own AI adoption still early-stage: 51% report limited use in the HR function and just 3% use it extensively, concentrated in recruiting (49%) and learning and development (41%). Across the whole business, 45% have implemented AI somewhere but only 26% call it fully operationalized. The report attributes the gap to skill gaps, unclear ownership, and uneven change readiness, and warns that without shared standards "work can move faster than accountability can keep up." Meanwhile retention has become the top HR priority for 57% of employers; 63% saw turnover of 10% or more in 2025 and 44% saw 15% or more.

So what · Put this next to the SAP and Salesforce items above and the picture is clear: vendors are shipping hundreds of agents into functions where nine in ten HR teams are still at "limited use." That's not a reason to wait; it's a reason to pick one workflow — recruiting screening or L&D content, where your peers already are — and operationalize it properly, with a named owner and a validation step, before adding a second. On retention: if turnover is a business risk at 57% of companies, HR's AI business case should be written in retention terms (manager time freed, faster internal moves) rather than headcount terms. It's the number the CFO is already watching.

Sources: HRD America · Gallagher 2026 US Workforce Trends Report

RegulationAmericas

The AI notetaker in your interviews is a compliance tool now — SHRM's CHRO: "have all of my ducks in a row"

Employment attorneys and SHRM CHRO Jim Link told HR Dive that AI meeting recorders and transcription tools — now routine in candidate interviews — sit at the intersection of three legal regimes: all-party-consent recording laws in states such as California, Florida and Illinois; state workplace-monitoring notice laws; and the growing set of laws regulating AI in employment decisions, which make a recorded, transcribed interview "more high risk than your typical Zoom meeting." Some tools deliberately don't announce that they're recording. Vendors' training-data practices vary and live in the service terms. Link says he gets two or three unsolicited AI-tool pitches a week and that any tool where the employer can't explain where candidate data comes from and what becomes of it "is not ready for hiring use."

So what · Inventory first: which notetakers are in use in recruiting, who turned them on, and do they announce themselves? Then adopt the most stringent jurisdiction's standard everywhere — all-party consent, spoken and logged at the top of every interview — because it's operationally simpler than a state-by-state matrix. Two contract questions for the vendor: is interview audio used to train models, and can we set retention to match our candidate-data policy? And note the connection to California's AB 1883 (still on Newsom's desk as of today): a transcript that's later run through sentiment or "engagement" scoring is emotion inference, and it's about to be regulated.

Sources: HR Dive · AP (notetaker boom) · Forkast (SB 947 status)
