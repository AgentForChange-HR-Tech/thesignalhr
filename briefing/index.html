The Briefing · Week of October 12, 2026

What changed this week — and what it means

Six signals worth your attention, each with the practitioner's "so what." Read in five minutes; sound informed for a week.

Curated by Alex RiveraUpdated October 15, 2026Refreshed weekly
Product

Workday puts agents on "autopilot": recruiting first, payroll next, plus an identity layer to govern them

At Workday Rising on October 13, Workday launched "Autopilot" agents that run in the background instead of waiting to be asked. The Talent Acquisition agent monitors open roles, flags those at risk of missing their fill date, surfaces candidates, drafts outreach for recruiter approval and coordinates interviews. It is available to early adopters now, with general availability planned for early 2027. A Payroll agent that checks for missing pay data before each run follows for early adopters in early 2027. Customers choose which actions run automatically and which need review, and every agent action is logged. The same day, Workday agreed to acquire C1.ai, which grants and revokes system access for people and AI agents; the deal is expected to close by January 31, 2027. Workday did not disclose pricing for agent activity.

So what · If you run Workday, this is the first agent wave aimed at your recruiting and payroll teams, and the questions to ask now are commercial and governance ones, not feature ones. Ask your account team three things: what early-adopter access costs and commits you to, how agent runs will be metered after general availability, and whether every candidate-facing step can be forced into human review. That last one will not stay optional: California's SB 947 requires a human to corroborate automated discipline and termination decisions from July 2027, and the EU AI Act's high-risk rules for employment AI apply from December 2027. The C1.ai deal is the sleeper story. Agents will need onboarding and offboarding just like people, so bring IT security into your agent pilot now rather than after go-live.

Sources: Workday Newsroom (Autopilot agents) · Workday Newsroom (C1.ai) · HR Dive

Regulation

California signs its workplace-AI laws: no firing by algorithm alone, no emotion-reading surveillance, and layoff notices that must name AI

Governor Newsom signed three workplace-AI bills on September 30. SB 947, the "No Robo Bosses Act," bars employers from relying solely on an automated decision system to discipline or fire someone. Where an employer primarily relies on one, a human must corroborate the output and the employee must get a written notice saying so; it takes effect July 1, 2027, with penalties of $500 per violation. AB 1883 bans AI workplace surveillance that infers employees' emotional states or collects neural data. SB 951 requires Cal-WARN notices to open with "This notice is for a technology displacement" when AI or automation is a substantial cause of layoffs, and to list the job functions being automated and the type of system involved. Both take effect January 1, 2027.

So what · Start with an inventory, because these laws reach beyond the HRIS: Morgan Lewis notes they cover general-purpose tools managers use on their own. For SB 947, map every point where an automated score or recommendation feeds discipline or termination, then write the human-corroboration step and the written-notice template well before July 2027. For SB 951, add one question to every California reduction-in-force checklist from January: did AI or automation substantially cause this? And check your monitoring tools for sentiment, "engagement" or emotion features now. AB 1883 makes them a liability on January 1, and that includes interview notetakers whose transcripts are later scored for tone.

Sources: Morgan Lewis · ITPro

Regulation

Connecticut's AI law is live: using an AI hiring tool is no longer a defense in a discrimination claim

Connecticut's CART Act (Public Act 26-15) began phasing in on October 1. From that date, an employer facing a direct discrimination claim involving an automated employment decision tool cannot use the tool as a defense, although regulators and courts may weigh anti-bias testing. Employers filing WARN notices must also tell the state Labor Department whether layoffs relate to AI or other technological change. The heavier obligations arrive October 1, 2027: plain-language disclosure when employees or applicants interact with AI, and written notice before AI output is used in an employment decision, naming the tool, its purpose and the data it analyzes. The attorney general enforces the law, with a 60-day cure period for violations through 2027.

So what · Two states, one pattern: the layoff notice is becoming an AI disclosure, and "the vendor's model decided" is no longer a defense. Decide now who answers the "did AI drive this?" question in a reduction in force, whether HR, legal or finance, and on what evidence, because the answer will be on file and quotable. The cheapest protection is the one Connecticut explicitly credits: documented anti-bias testing of every screening tool you use. Ask vendors for their latest results and the tool details the 2027 notices will require, in writing, at your next renewal.

Sources: Ropes & Gray · Cohen and Wolf

Workforce

Hiring nearly stalled in September, and AI dropped to fifth among layoff reasons while remaining 2026's top cause

The US economy added just 29,000 jobs in September and unemployment rose to 4.2%, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported October 2. July and August were revised down by a combined 60,000. Announced layoffs eased: Challenger, Gray & Christmas counted 43,281 cuts in September, down 18% from August and 20% from a year earlier. AI was cited for 3,961 of them, about 9%, making it the fifth most-cited reason for the month. It remains the leading reason for the year, at 120,136 cuts or about 21% of all announced cuts. Employers announced 90,787 planned hires, most of them in retail.

So what · Few layoffs plus near-zero hiring is a frozen market, and it changes the AI conversation inside your company. With fewer exits and fewer hires, the productivity leaders expect from AI has to come from redesigning existing roles, not from attrition or replacement. That makes it an internal-mobility and reskilling brief for HR, not a headcount one. Keep an eye on the monthly AI share of cuts: it has fallen out of the top three reasons two months running, which is useful evidence for any board conversation that treats AI layoffs as inevitable.

Sources: BLS Employment Situation · Challenger Report, September 2026

Talent

Gartner: only 24% of leadership teams agree on who is accountable for AI agents

Gartner's CHRO priorities for 2027, released October 6, put "human accountability for AI" second on the list. In a July 2026 survey of 297 CHROs, only 24% said their leadership teams agree on who is accountable for AI agents. The other priorities are delivering value faster, focusing on fewer, differentiating skills (67% of CHROs report higher productivity expectations) and preparing leaders for disruption: 63% of CEOs and senior executives surveyed in May say they are radically redesigning their operating models to speed AI returns. Gartner also warns that agents don't just eliminate work; they push ambiguity, judgment calls and recovery tasks onto employees.

So what · This is the number that ties the rest of this edition together. Workday's autopilots, California's corroboration rule and Connecticut's disclosure rules all assume someone owns the agent, and in three of four leadership teams nobody has agreed who does. Put a one-page agent accountability charter on your next leadership agenda: for each agent in use or in pilot, name the business owner, the human reviewer, the escalation path and who answers when it is wrong. HR is the natural author, because it already runs the equivalent for people. Gartner's point about judgment calls is the same one in our analysis of the judgment premium.

Sources: Gartner

Market

HR tech funding rebounded to $346M in Q3, but HiBob's single round was nearly half of it

HR tech venture funding reached $346M across 37 rounds in Q3 2026, up from $225M in Q2, according to Kyle Getsiv's quarterly review. HiBob's $166M round from Salesforce, at a reported $3.2B valuation, accounted for nearly half; without it, the quarter came in below Q2. Only two US Series A rounds were announced, the fewest since 2020, and the median seed round was the smallest since 2020. Other notable rounds included Metaview ($60M Series C) and Miter ($40M Series B).

So what · For buyers, this is a market of well-funded incumbents and thinly funded newcomers, and your diligence should reflect that. When a startup is on your shortlist, ask about runway, the next funding milestone and what happens to your data if it is acquired or shuts down; the RFP question bank in our ATS comparison covers the data-exit question. When an incumbent is, remember why capital is flowing: investors are betting on workforce data as the context AI agents need. Your HR data is what makes their agents valuable, so negotiate data-use and model-training terms as carefully as price.

Sources: Kyle Getsiv, Q3 Funding Review · HR Tech Feed
